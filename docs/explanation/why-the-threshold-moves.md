# Why the long-word threshold has to move

```
LIX = A/B + (C · 100)/A
```

The second term is the share of running words that are "long", as a percentage. Björnsson
fixed the boundary at more than six letters, fitted to Swedish. Every Python implementation
hardcodes that six.

## What the six was for

For Swedish it is a reasonable cut. Measured over Leipzig's `swe_news_2022_1M`, 25.65% of
running Swedish words are longer than six letters — roughly the longest quarter. The term
therefore varies usefully between texts: a document with unusually long words stands out
against a base rate that leaves room in both directions.

## What it does elsewhere

Over the full Hungarian Webcorpus frequency list, 44.52% of running words are longer than
six. Nearly half of everything is "long". The second term sits high for *every* text, so it
stops distinguishing them, and a per-decade or per-book plot flattens into a line.

That is not a claim about Hungarian being harder to read. It is an artefact of measuring an
agglutinative language with a boundary fitted to a Germanic one. Ancient Greek behaves the
same way for the same reason.

## Preserving the meaning rather than the number

There are two things one could keep constant across languages: the literal threshold, or what
the threshold *selects*. Björnsson's six selects about the longest quarter of running Swedish
words, and that — "the longest quarter" — is what the second LIX term is actually about.

**Equipercentile matching** keeps the second. Find the threshold whose long-word share in
your language is closest to the share the reference threshold picks out in the reference
language. For Hungarian that is eight, which selects 27.33% against Swedish's 25.65%.

The alternative — keeping the six and accepting that the term means something different in
each language — is defensible only if you never compare across languages. Since comparing is
usually the point, it is not much of an option.

## Why one corpus was not enough

A threshold derived from a single corpus is a fact about that corpus. The Hungarian
Webcorpus is a winter-2003 web crawl, so a number from it could easily be an artefact of
2000s web writing rather than of the language.

Running the same method over Leipzig's 2022 Hungarian news corpus — a different corpus, built
by a different project, nineteen years later — gives shares within about three points at
every threshold, and the same recommended threshold. That agreement is the actual evidence.
The number 8 is only as good as the reason to believe it, and one corpus is not a reason.

## Why the answer comes with a bracket

`ThresholdRecommendation` reports the runner-up and whether the choice is contested, which
looks like over-engineering until you see it earn its place.

Run against a truncated sample — the hundred thousand most frequent words, about 95% of
tokens — the 8%-stratum and full-corpus columns chose **seven**, and the 4% stratum and
Leipzig chose eight. The choice really was close to arbitrary. The missing 5% is the long
tail, and including it pushed the Hungarian shares up and made eight unanimous.

A bare integer would have concealed that entirely. It is also why the deliverable is the
whole curve rather than the winner: someone who disagrees with the Swedish reference can read
their own answer off the same table.

## Why the bands stop applying

Re-calibrating rescales the `100·C/A` term by construction. A Hungarian LIX at threshold
eight is simply not on the scale Björnsson fitted his labels to, so `LixResult.band` returns
`None` there rather than hand out a Swedish label for a Hungarian number.

This is structural rather than a warning in prose, on the principle that a docstring nobody
reads is not a guard. `interpret_lix(score)` remains available for anyone who wants a label
anyway — deliberately, and at the call site, where the decision is visible.

## What a calibrated threshold is not

It is a **default, not a truth**, and it is specific to a register. The Webcorpus is 2000s
web Hungarian: not song lyrics, not literary prose, not administrative writing. Before
trusting eight on a different register, the honest move is to recompute the long-word share
on a sample of the actual target texts and check it lands near 27%.

There is also a limit worth naming. `recommended_threshold` reads a literal generated by the
study, and nothing verifies that this literal still matches the JSON it came from — the
corpora are not distributed, so nothing *can*. The guard against a stray edit is only that
the same numbers also live in `findings.md` and in the results JSON, where a discrepancy
would show.

## Digraphs, and the limits of being clever

Hungarian `cs, dz, gy, ly, ny, sz, ty, zs, dzs` are single letters, so a character count is
not a letter count. It is tempting to conclude that the length policy should collapse them.

The calibration recomputes on letters as a sensitivity check and still chooses eight. But
collapsing is not straightforwardly *better*: it misfires wherever a digraph spans a morpheme
boundary, so `község` — `köz` + `ség` — is counted as five letters when it is six. There is
no way to know which is which without morphological analysis, which returns us to the
dependency saphes declines to take.

So character counting stays the default, as in the original, and the letter count is offered
as a way to check whether a conclusion survives the choice. That is the honest status of
almost everything in this study: not a better number, but a known one.
